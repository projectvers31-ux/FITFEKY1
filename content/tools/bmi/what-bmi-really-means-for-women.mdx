BMI is fast, free, and a half-century old. It's also the single most-cited health metric on the planet — and one of the most misunderstood. If you've ever punched your height and weight into a calculator, watched a coloured bar flash up, and thought *"yeah, but…"* — you're right. The story is bigger than the number.

This guide is the long version. It's what we wish every BMI tool actually told you. Read it once, and you'll never look at a BMI chart the same way.

## The formula, in one line

Body Mass Index is a simple ratio: weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared.

> **BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height (m)²**

A 70 kg woman at 1.65 m has a BMI of 25.7 — just inside the "overweight" band, even though her body composition is perfectly normal. That's because the formula was never built to capture her.

## Why BMI was designed for populations, not people

Adolphe Quetelet, a 19th-century Belgian mathematician, created the index in 1832 to describe the *average* build of a population — not to diagnose individuals. It wasn't meant to be a personal verdict.

When life-insurance companies adopted it in the mid-20th century, they did so because it was cheap and easy. They weren't optimising for accuracy; they were optimising for cost. That decision calcified into clinical practice, and the rest is history.

## What BMI gets right

- **It anchors a conversation.** "Where do I sit on the standard scale?" is a useful starting point.
- **It tracks population trends.** Public-health agencies use it to track obesity rates over decades.
- **It's free and instant.** No equipment, no lab, no appointment.

## What BMI gets wrong for women

- **It ignores muscle.** A woman who lifts heavy 4× a week can easily read "overweight" while carrying 25% body fat.
- **It ignores bone density.** Smaller-framed women lose bone earlier in life; the index doesn't adjust.
- **It ignores hormonal life stages.** Perimenopause, postpartum, and the luteal phase all shift the number by water weight alone.
- **It ignores ethnicity.** The same BMI cut-offs predict different health risks for South Asian, East Asian, and Black women compared with white European women.

## The four checks that tell you more than BMI

If you want a fuller picture in 60 seconds, pair BMI with these:

1. **Waist circumference.** Measure at the navel, relaxed. Under 80 cm is low risk for women; over 88 cm is high risk.
2. **Resting heart rate.** Lower usually means fitter cardiovascular system. 60–80 bpm is typical for women; trained athletes sit in the 50s.
3. **Strength-to-weight ratio.** Can you do 5 pull-ups? Carry two suitcases upstairs? Move freely?
4. **How you feel.** Energy, sleep, cycle regularity, mood — these matter more than any single number.
