If you've been told to "just lose weight" for your PCOS, you've been given the worst possible advice. PCOS is a metabolic disorder driven primarily by insulin resistance — and the same insulin resistance is what makes weight loss harder for you than for someone without PCOS.

The good news: it's also the most treatable aspect of the condition.

## What PCOS actually is

Polycystic Ovary Syndrome affects roughly 1 in 10 women of reproductive age. The diagnostic criteria (Rotterdam) require 2 of 3:
- Irregular or absent ovulation
- Elevated androgens (testosterone, DHEA-S) — clinical signs like acne, hirsutism, or a blood test
- Polycystic ovaries on ultrasound

The hormonal cascade: **insulin resistance → hyperinsulinemia → increased ovarian androgen production → disrupted follicle development → irregular cycles and fertility issues.**

Insulin is the upstream lever. Everything else is downstream.

## Why "just lose 5%" is half-true, half-bullshit

It *is* true that a 5–10% weight loss can restore ovulation in many women with PCOS. It is also true that PCOS makes losing 5% biologically harder. So "just lose weight" is technically correct advice delivered in a way that is unhelpful and often cruel.

Better framing: **address the insulin resistance, and weight loss becomes possible (and often happens as a side effect).**

## The 4 interventions with the strongest evidence

### 1. Strength training
A 2022 meta-analysis in *Human Reproduction Update* found that resistance training in women with PCOS:
- Reduced fasting insulin by 12–18%
- Improved HOMA-IR scores (a key insulin resistance marker)
- Reduced androgens
- Restored menstrual regularity in ~50% of participants

You don't need to lose weight first. Start training at the body you have now.

### 2. Lower glycemic load, not lower calories
Calorie restriction alone has poor long-term outcomes in PCOS. Reducing glycemic load — the total glucose-raising impact of your meals — has better results:

- Replace refined grains with legumes, oats, and whole grains
- Front-load carbs around training
- Pair carbs with protein and fat
- Consider a moderate carbohydrate approach (30–40% of calories) rather than very-low-carb

The goal isn't ketosis. It's avoiding the glucose → insulin → androgen spike.

### 3. Inositol (myo-inositol + D-chiro-inositol)
This is the supplement with the strongest evidence for PCOS. A 2018 meta-analysis found that myo-inositol (2 g/day) combined with D-chiro-inositol improved:
- Ovulation rates
- Androgen levels
- Insulin sensitivity
- Pregnancy rates in some studies

It is not a magic pill, but it is the most-studied and most-promising supplement for PCOS specifically. Talk to your doctor before starting.

### 4. Sleep and stress work
Cortisol elevation worsens insulin resistance. Sleep deprivation raises insulin the next day by 20–30%. Both are modifiable.

## What about metformin?

Metformin is sometimes prescribed for PCOS, and it works. But lifestyle changes have similar effects on insulin sensitivity with fewer side effects. If you're already on metformin, don't stop — talk to your doctor about whether it's still serving you. If you're not, lifestyle first is a reasonable starting point.

## A 12-week starter plan

| Week | Focus |
|---|---|
| 1–2 | Sleep 7+ hours; protein at every meal; daily 20-min walk |
| 3–4 | Add 2 strength sessions per week (full body, RPE 7) |
| 5–8 | Add a third strength session; meal-prep to lower glycemic load |
| 9–12 | Add zone-2 cardio; evaluate inositol with your doctor; reassess |

## The bottom line

PCOS is not a failure of willpower. It's a metabolic condition with a clear upstream cause (insulin resistance) and clear levers (strength training, glycemic load, sleep, stress, inositol).

Stop trying to "just eat less" and start addressing the actual problem.
