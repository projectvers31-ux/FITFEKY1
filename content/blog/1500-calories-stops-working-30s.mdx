If you've ever cut to 1,200 or 1,500 calories, lost a few pounds, and then watched the scale freeze for weeks — you're not broken. The advice was.

The "1,500 calorie" rule of thumb is roughly 25–30% below the *average* maintenance intake for a 25-year-old man weighing 70 kg. It has never been calibrated for the realities of female metabolism: lower lean mass, cycle-driven energy shifts, the metabolic cost of sleep disruption, and the hormonal landscape that changes every decade from the mid-20s on.

## The math your calorie app isn't doing

Most calculators — including ours — use **Mifflin-St Jeor**, the most accurate validated equation for resting energy expenditure. It's a starting point, not a verdict. From there we add an activity multiplier (1.2–1.9) to get Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE). A moderate deficit is 15–25% below TDEE.

For a 35-year-old woman at 165 cm, 68 kg, lightly active:
- TDEE ≈ 1,950 kcal
- 25% deficit = **1,460 kcal**

That's barely 1,500 — and that's the floor of *moderate* deficit, not aggressive. The 1,200-calorie prescriptions you see circulating are not moderate; they're a 40% cut, and they come with consequences.

## What actually happens on 1,200 calories

Three things start breaking within 2–3 weeks:

1. **Lean mass loss accelerates.** A 2010 meta-analysis in *Obesity Reviews* found that very low-calorie diets (under 1,200 kcal) result in ~25% of weight lost as muscle — versus 10–15% on moderate deficits.
2. **Resting metabolism drops more than predicted.** Adaptive thermogenesis (the body's "I'll just run colder" response) is roughly 5–10% on moderate deficits but can hit 15%+ on aggressive ones.
3. **Hormones shift in fat-retaining directions.** Leptin falls, ghrelin rises, cortisol climbs, and — most relevant for women — **thyroid hormone T3 drops**, slowing metabolism further.

## A better approach for women over 30

### 1. Start with your real TDEE, not a number from the internet
Use our [calorie calculator](/tools/calorie) and then track honestly for 2–3 weeks *without* dieting. Your actual weight-maintenance calories are often 100–200 above what the formula predicts.

### 2. Cut by 15%, not 30%
If you maintain at 2,000, eat 1,700 — not 1,400. You'll lose fat a little slower and keep your muscle, your cycle, and your sanity.

### 3. Cycle your calories with your menstrual cycle
The luteal phase (the 7–10 days before your period) burns 100–300 extra kcal per day. Eating the same amount every day means relative over-eating in the follicular phase and under-eating in the luteal phase. Match intake to phase.

### 4. Recalculate every 4–6 weeks
As you lose weight, TDEE drops. Recheck. If your weight has stalled for 2+ weeks, reduce by another 100 kcal — or, better, add one strength session.

### 5. Protect protein and sleep above all else
Protein at 1.6–2.0 g/kg preserves muscle. Sleep under 7 hours tanks insulin sensitivity the next day — undoing your deficit with one night of poor rest.

## The bottom line

"1,500 calories" is a meme. Your real deficit is a moving target that depends on your cycle, your sleep, your training age, and how much muscle you have. Track, recalculate, and treat the number as a hypothesis — not a religion.
